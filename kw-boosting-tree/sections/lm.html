Lexicon matching corrects the transcript once Whisper has written it: it reads the transcript's words, looks for the ones that are close to a keyterm of the lexicon by spelling or by sound, and replaces them with the keyterm. It uses no LLM and never sees the audio. LLM post-processing does the same job with an LLM, which reads the transcript and the lexicon and rewrites the text; it is the heavy option, outside this report.

What lexicon matching can fix is a keyterm Whisper wrote close to its right form: misspelled (« Rivoly » for « Rivoli »), or written as ordinary words that sound alike (« le clerc » for « Leclerc »). A keyterm Whisper wrote as words that look and sound nothing like it is out of its reach.

Which words are checked

A keyterm can sit anywhere in the transcript and span one word or several, so lexicon matching checks runs of words. A word n-gram is a run of n consecutive words of the transcript. A candidate span is a word n-gram checked against the lexicon as a possibly misrecognized keyterm. The sliding window takes every word n-gram of the transcript in turn, for each n around a keyterm's length.

The n has to vary around the keyterm's own length because of the word boundary error: Whisper writes a keyterm as more or fewer words than it has, one word split in two (« Leclerc » as « le clerc ») or two words merged. A one-word keyterm is then found in a span of two words. Before any comparison, both sides go through text normalization: lowercasing, removing punctuation and accents, writing numbers alike.

The cost follows from the counts. A transcript of 9 words gives 9 + 8 + 7 + 6 = 30 candidate spans for n from 1 to 4; compared with each of 1,000 keyterms, that is 30,000 comparisons for one utterance. Once each comparison has a score, the best keyterm of each span is kept, and where two kept spans overlap (« le clerc » and « char le clerc ») the one with the better score wins.
