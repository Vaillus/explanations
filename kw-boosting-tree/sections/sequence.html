No paper runs the boosting tree and then lexicon matching on its transcript. One paper runs the same two stages with a trained corrector in place of lexicon matching: a bias added while the model writes, then a correction of its transcript, each alone and both in sequence. It is the closest measure of the plan.

Wang, Liu, Li, Miljanic, Zhao and Khalil, « Towards Contextual Spelling Correction for Customization of End-to-end Speech Recognition Systems », arXiv 2203.00888, 2022 (Microsoft). The first stage is a list of phrases built into a weighted graph and added by shallow fusion, of the same family as the boosting tree. The second is contextual spelling correction: a small trained model that reads the transcript and the list, and rewrites the spans it takes for misrecognized phrases. Before it scores, the list is cut to the 100 phrases closest to the transcript. RNN-T models, English, Microsoft's test sets.

On the sets where list phrases are said, the sequence gives the lowest WER on four of five, and on the fifth (the 210 commands of a text editor) the corrector alone does better than both. On the set where no phrase of the list is said, each stage adds its own false alarms: 13.9 alone, 14.0 with the bias, 14.4 with the corrector or both. The paper reports neither B-WER nor U-WER, so how much of each gain is recovered phrases and how much is ordinary words replaced cannot be read.

The second stage reads a transcript the first already biased: keyterms the boosting tree wrote are spelled as in the lexicon and score 1 against it, and what is left to match is what the tree did not recover. The false alarms of both stages reach the same U-WER, so the boosting weight and the match threshold are tuned together, on the transcripts of the tree.
