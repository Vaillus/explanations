Each candidate span is given a score against a keyterm, and the score says how likely the span is a misrecognition of it. Two families of scores exist, one on spelling and one on sound, and they catch different errors.

By spelling: edit distance

The edit distance (Levenshtein distance) is the number of character insertions, deletions and substitutions that turn one string into another: « rivoly » is at 1 from « rivoli ». Divided by the length of the longer string, it gives a score from 0 to 1 that keyterms of any length can share.

By sound: phonetic matching

Whisper's errors on a rare word are often words that sound like it and are spelled otherwise: « le clair » for « Leclerc », « cervale » for « Serval ». The spelling is far, the sound is the same. Phonetic matching compares two strings by how they sound: each is turned into a phonetic code, a string that encodes the pronunciation so that words spelled differently but pronounced alike get the same or close codes, and the codes are compared, exactly or by edit distance.

Two kinds of phonetic code exist. A rule-based key, computed from the spelling by a fixed set of rules (Soundex, Metaphone for English; Phonex, FONEM, a French Soundex for French), is fast and needs no model, and is crude. A string of phonemes, the units of sound that tell words apart in a language, written in a notation such as IPA, is finer: it comes from grapheme-to-phoneme conversion (G2P), a dictionary or a model such as eSpeak that predicts the phonemes of a written word. G2P on proper names and foreign words is where it fails most, and keyterms are mostly such words.

The rules of a phonetic code are those of one language. The transcripts are French, and keyterms such as foreign weapon or place names mix languages, so the code and the G2P are chosen for French and checked on the lexicon before any measurement. The two scores can be combined: a span is taken as a keyterm when either is high enough, or on a weighted sum of both.
