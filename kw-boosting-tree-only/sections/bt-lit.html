Four papers measure the boosting tree as sections 5 to 10 describe it: a prefix tree of keyterm tokens, a boosting weight added by shallow fusion before pruning, and withdrawal. One of them runs it on Whisper. The others run it on three other families of speech-to-text models: CTC models, which score every audio frame independently and need no decoder; RNN-T models (transducers), which add a small decoder fed with the tokens already written and are common in streaming; and AED models (attention encoder-decoder), the family of Whisper, whose decoder writes token by token while attending to the whole audio. Run time is given as RTFx: seconds of audio transcribed per second of computation.

TurboBias adds two findings. Beam search gains far more than greedy decoding (17 to 23 F-score points against 8 to 10), since several hypotheses can carry a keyterm until its last token. And a boosting weight that grows with depth in the tree, c0·β + ln(depth) for tokens past the first, beats a uniform weight with a reward at completion: on CTC with beam search, F-score 83.2 against 75.0 (Table III).

gaps

No paper measures the boosting tree on a Whisper finetuned on the domain of its keyterms, and none on French. The one Whisper result is a preprint on read English audiobooks, at the lexicon size of the sprint. A paper that combines a prefix tree with a trained neural module on Whisper (Lall and Liu, arXiv 2410.18363, 2024) needs training, so it is a different technique.
